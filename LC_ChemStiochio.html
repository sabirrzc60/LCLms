Level 0 • Foundations

ZERO: Reading the Language of Chemistry

Before writing or balancing any reaction, you must read chemical shorthand accurately. Every symbol tells you what particles are present and what physical state they occupy.

1. Elements, Compounds, Mixtures & Building Blocks

An element contains only one type of atom (such as iron, Fe, or oxygen, O2). A compound contains two or more different elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio (such as water, H2O, or sodium chloride, NaCl). A mixture combines substances physically without fixed chemical bonds (such as air or salt water).

Atom & Molecule

An atom is a single neutral particle (Ne). A molecule is two or more non-metal atoms covalently bonded (H2O, CO2).

Ion

An atom or group of atoms with a net electrical charge: cations are positive (Na+, Ca2+) and anions are negative (Cl−, SO42−).

Formula Unit

Ionic solids form giant crystal lattices rather than discrete molecules. NaCl or Ca(NO3)2 shows the simplest whole-number ion ratio (formula unit).

2. Diatomic Elements ("HONClBrIF" / "7-Up" Trick)

Seven common non-metal elements exist naturally as diatomic molecules (two atoms bonded together) when uncombined in their standard elemental state: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2. Pronounce “HON-kel-brif” (H, O, N, Cl, Br, I, F), or spot the 7-shaped cluster starting at element 7 (Nitrogen) across to F, Cl, Br, I on the periodic table (plus Hydrogen at #1).

Common Beginner Trap When a word equation says “hydrogen reacts with chlorine to form hydrogen chloride gas”, write H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g) — NOT H + Cl!

3. Common Polyatomic Ions to Know Cold

Polyatomic ions act as single charged packets in many reactions. Keep them intact when balancing unless they break apart.

4. State Symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq)

  • (s) Solid: Metals (except Hg), ionic crystals, precipitates (insoluble solids formed in solution).
  • (l) Liquid: Pure molten substances or pure liquids such as liquid water H2O(l), ethanol C2H5OH(l), or bromine Br2(l).
  • (g) Gas: Gases such as O2(g), CO2(g), H2(g), NH3(g), HCl(g).
  • (aq) Aqueous: Dissolved in water! Acids like HCl(aq), H2SO4(aq), alkalis like NaOH(aq), and soluble salts like NaCl(aq) are aqueous solutions unless stated as solid or molten.

Level 1 • Formula Construction

LEVEL 1: Writing Correct Chemical Formulas First

The #1 Rule Before Balancing: Every chemical formula must be chemically valid before you add big front coefficients. Subscripts fix the internal recipe of a compound; changing a subscript changes the substance itself!

Golden Rule of Balancing Water is H2O because oxygen has charge −2 and each hydrogen has charge +1. Never turn H2O into H2O2 (hydrogen peroxide, a completely different chemical!) just to make oxygen atoms match. Always use coefficients in front of formulas.

1. Ionic Compounds: Charge Balance & Swap-and-Drop

In a neutral ionic compound, total positive charge equals total negative charge. Using swap-and-drop, take the magnitude of each ion’s charge and drop it as the opposite subscript, then simplify to the smallest whole-number ratio.

Worked Examples
  • Magnesium chloride: Mg2+ + Cl− → swap magnitudes (2 and 1) → MgCl2.
  • Calcium nitrate (polyatomic ion): Ca2+ + NO3− → need two nitrates for one Ca2+ → put parentheses around the polyatomic ion: Ca(NO3)2. (Without parentheses, CaNO32 would be wrong!)
  • Iron(III) oxide (Roman numeral = charge +3): Fe3+ + O2− → Fe2O3.
  • Tin(IV) oxide: Sn4+ + O2− → Sn2O4 → simplify ratio 2:4 to 1:2 → SnO2.

2. Molecular / Covalent Prefixes & Common Acids

For two non-metals (covalent molecules), Greek prefixes often tell you the subscripts directly: mono- (1), di- (2), tri- (3), tetra- (4), penta- (5), hexa- (6). Example: dinitrogen pentoxide = N2O5; sulfur hexafluoride = SF6.

Common acids you will meet constantly: hydrochloric acid HCl(aq), sulfuric acid H2SO4(aq), nitric acid HNO3(aq), phosphoric acid H3PO4(aq), and ethanoic/acetic acid CH3COOH(aq).

Level 2 • Translation & Patterns

LEVEL 2: Word Equations → Skeleton (Unbalanced) Equations

A skeleton equation translates reaction words into chemical formulas with state symbols and an arrow (→), showing what reacts and what forms before atom counts are equalized.

1. Reading Reaction Cue Words

  • Reactants (Left of →): “A reacts with B”, “when X is added to Y”, “heating Z”, “burning in air/oxygen”.
  • Products (Right of →): “to produce / yield / form / give”, “leaving behind”, “evolving gas”.
  • Conditions & States: “aqueous / dissolved in water” → (aq); “precipitate forms” → (s); “gas bubbles off” → (g).

Level 3 • Atom Accounting

LEVEL 3: Balancing Equations Step-by-Step

Matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction (Law of Conservation of Mass). Every atom on the reactant side must appear on the product side.

Systematic 4-Step Balancing Method

  1. Verify formulas first: Confirm every subscript is right; never change subscripts while balancing.
  2. Tally atoms (and intact polyatomic ions): Count atoms on left and right. If a polyatomic ion like SO42− or NO3− stays together on both sides, count it as one unit!
  3. Balance the most complex or least common element first: Tackle metals and distinct non-metals before hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O), especially elemental O2.
  4. Audit & reduce to lowest whole-number ratio: Recount every single atom on both sides. If all coefficients share a common factor (e.g., 2, 4, 2), divide through to 1, 2, 1.
Worked Walkthrough: Propane Combustion
Skeleton: C3H8(g) + O2(g) → CO2(g) + H2O(g)

Step 3a (Carbon): 3 C on left → put 3 before CO2.

Step 3b (Hydrogen): 8 H on left → put 4 before H2O (since 4 × 2 = 8).

Step 3c (Oxygen last): Right now has (3 × 2) + (4 × 1) = 10 O atoms. Divide 10 by 2 → put 5 before O2.

Balanced: C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(g)  [Check: 3 C, 8 H, 10 O each side]

Level 4 • Ionic & Net Ionic

LEVEL 4: Net Ionic Equations & Spectator Ions

In aqueous reactions, soluble strong electrolytes exist as separated ions. Writing a net ionic equation strips away spectator ions (ions that appear unchanged on both sides) to highlight the actual chemical change.

1. Solubility Intuition & Strong Electrolytes

  • Generally soluble in water: All Group 1 (Li+, Na+, K+) and ammonium (NH4+) salts; all nitrates (NO3−); most sulfates (except BaSO4, PbSO4, CaSO4 slightly soluble); most chlorides/bromides/iodides (except Ag+, Pb2+, Hg22+).
  • Common precipitates (s): AgCl(s), BaSO4(s), PbI2(s), CaCO3(s), Fe(OH)3(s).
  • Split into ions when (aq): Strong acids (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, H2SO4), strong soluble bases (NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)2), and soluble salts. Do NOT split solids (s), pure liquids (l) like H2O(l), gases (g), or weak acids/bases.
Worked Example: Silver Nitrate + Sodium Chloride
Molecular: AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)
Complete Ionic: Ag+(aq) + NO3−(aq) + Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s) + Na+(aq) + NO3−(aq)

Spectators (unchanged on both sides): Na+(aq) and NO3−(aq).

Net Ionic: Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s)

Level 5 • Hero Stoichiometry

LEVEL 5 (HERO): Moles, Mole Ratios & Stoichiometric Calculations

Balanced coefficients give mole ratios, NOT gram ratios! You can only convert from Substance A to Substance B through moles (n).

1. Core Mole Equations (Note Standard Conditions)

Mass & Molar Mass
n = m / M

n = moles (mol), m = mass (g), M = molar mass (g·mol−1).

Gas Volume (Assumed)
n = V / Vm

Assumes ideal gas: 22.4 L·mol−1 at STP (0 °C, 1 atm) or 24.0 dm3·mol−1 at RTP (25 °C, 1 bar).

Solution Molarity
n = c × V

c = molarity (mol·L−1 or M), V = solution volume in litres (L) (mL ÷ 1000).

2. The 4-Step Stoichiometry Roadmap

Given Unit → Moles of Given → × (Target Coeff / Given Coeff) → Moles of Target → Desired Unit
Worked Example: Mass of CO2 from Burning 8.80 g of Propane (C3H8)
C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(g)

1. Moles of C3H8: M(C3H8) = 3(12.01) + 8(1.008) = 44.09 g·mol−1. So n(C3H8) = 8.80 / 44.09 = 0.200 mol.

2. Mole ratio (CO2 / C3H8): 3 mol CO2 per 1 mol C3H8 → n(CO2) = 0.200 × (3 / 1) = 0.600 mol.

3. Mass of CO2: M(CO2) = 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01 g·mol−1. So m(CO2) = 0.600 × 44.01 = 26.4 g.

3. Limiting Reagent & Percent Yield

When given amounts of two reactants, the limiting reagent runs out first and caps the maximum (theoretical) yield of product; the other is in excess. To find the limiting reagent cleanly, divide each reactant’s moles by its balanced coefficient — the smallest quotient is limiting!

Percent Yield (%) = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) × 100%

Walkthrough: Suppose theoretical yield of CaO is 28.0 g, and your lab experiment isolates 23.8 g. Percent yield = (23.8 / 28.0) × 100% = 85.0%. (Yield < 100% often comes from incomplete reaction, side reactions, or transfer losses.)
